Clearing out a parent's house isn't a chore. It's forty years of somebody's life laid out on a kitchen table, and you're the one who has to decide what happens to it.

We can't make that part easier. What we can do is take the heavy, dusty, logistical half off your back so you've got room for the rest of it. Here's what we've learned doing [estate cleanouts across the Jacksonville Beaches](/services/estate-cleanouts): the order that works, the mistakes that cost people, and the things worth slowing down for.

## Before you touch anything

Don't start with a truck. Start with paperwork.

Find out who the personal representative is. In Florida, that's the person named in the will (or appointed by the court) with the say over what leaves the house. Not sure there's a will? Check the desk, the filing cabinet, the safe, and with whoever handled the family's legal work. Until that's settled, the safest move is to move nothing.

Then talk to the estate's attorney before the first bag goes out. Rules about what needs to be inventoried or accounted for vary, and we're a junk removal company — not lawyers. Ask them. It's a short conversation that saves longer ones later.

And if anything might be genuinely valuable — a collection, artwork, a piece of furniture that always seemed like more than furniture — get an appraiser's eyes on it first.

## Give yourself a timeline, and don't do it alone

Most families underestimate this by half. A modest three-bedroom with an attic and a full garage is not a weekend. It's several — and that's if nobody stops to read old letters, which everybody does, and should.

If the house is being sold or the lease is ending, work backward from that date and build in slack. If there's no deadline, don't invent one. Some families do a room a month for a year. That's fine.

Bring people. Not for the labor — for the company. Sorting through someone's belongings alone in a quiet house is a particular kind of hard, and there's no medal for it.

## The four-pile method

Every room, four categories. Keep. Donate. Recycle. Haul.

The method is simple. The deciding is not. A few things that help.

**Ask "would I buy this today?"** Not "was this important to Mom?" Everything was important to somebody. The question is whether it has a life ahead of it.

**Take a photo instead.** You can keep the memory of the recliner without keeping the recliner. This is the single most useful trick we know.

**Set a maybe box, and cap it.** One box per person. When it's full, it's full.

**Decide once.** If you pick something up, put it in a pile. Don't set it back down to think about later. Later never comes, and by hour six you're just moving objects around the floor.

Our crew handles the last three piles. The keep pile is yours, and we don't touch it unless you say so.

## Where the valuables hide

People of a certain generation didn't trust banks with everything. Before anything gets hauled, search:

- **Books.** Cash, savings bonds, and photographs get tucked between pages. Fan every book.
- **Coat pockets, purses, shoe boxes.** Jewelry, rings, cash.
- **The freezer, the flour tin, the toilet tank.** We're serious.
- **Dresser drawers — undersides and backs included.** Envelopes get taped there.
- **Toolboxes and workbenches.** Old hand tools are often worth real money. So is the copper and brass in those coffee cans.
- **War memorabilia.** Medals, discharge papers, letters home. These matter to families more than almost anything else in the house, and they're small enough to vanish in a box marked "junk."
- **Photo albums and loose negatives.** Attics and garages swallow these.

This is part of why we walk the house with you before we lift a thing. We've found sealed envelopes taped behind a dresser during a [garage cleanout](/services/garage-cleanouts), and coins in a shed nobody had opened in years.

## What to never throw out without checking

Some things are hard to replace. Others are impossible.

- **Documents.** Deeds, titles, mortgage paperwork, insurance policies, military records, tax returns, birth and marriage certificates. If it looks official, box it and hand it to the personal representative.
- **Photos and film.** Even unlabeled ones. Somebody in the family will know the faces.
- **Anything with an account number on it.** Shred it or hand it over. Don't bag it.
- **Medications.** These need proper disposal — not the trash, not the toilet. Many pharmacies and sheriff's offices run take-back boxes.
- **Keys you can't identify.** Safe deposit boxes, storage units, a shed nobody remembered.

Rule of thumb: paper goes in a box, not a bag. Sort it later, somewhere quiet.

## Room by room, in the order that works

Don't start in the bedroom. Everyone wants to, and it's the room that'll stop you cold on day one.

Start where the emotional weight is lightest and the volume is heaviest.

1. **Garage, lanai, shed.** Paint cans, lawn chairs, that treadmill. Mostly obvious calls. You'll build momentum fast.
2. **Attic.** Heavier on memories, but still mostly boxes. Check every one — and it gets hot up there fast in a Florida summer, so work it early in the day.
3. **Kitchen, bathrooms, laundry.** Practical, quick, and you'll clear a lot of square footage.
4. **Living and dining rooms.** Furniture and appliances — the big stuff that needs a truck.
5. **Bedrooms and the closet.** Last. By now you've had practice, and you'll be steadier than you'd have been on day one.

## The estate cleanout checklist

If you want the whole thing on one screen, here it is. Work it top to bottom and you won't miss the parts that matter. It holds up anywhere along the Beaches, whether the house is a cottage in Jacksonville Beach, a condo near the pier, or a home out in Ponte Vedra or Nocatee.

- [ ] Find the will and confirm who the personal representative is before anything leaves the house.
- [ ] Set a realistic timeline and line up help. Don't plan to do it alone in one weekend.
- [ ] Locate and set aside every document: deeds, titles, insurance policies, tax records, military papers.
- [ ] Search for hidden valuables: cash tucked in books, jewelry, coins, old photos, tools, anything collectible.
- [ ] Pull out medications and dispose of them properly, not in the trash.
- [ ] Sort everything into four piles: keep, donate, recycle, haul.
- [ ] Start with the least emotional room. Garage, lanai, or a spare room, not the bedroom.
- [ ] Photograph anything of value for the estate records or the personal representative before it goes.
- [ ] Confirm donation pickups and what each center will and won't take that week.
- [ ] Book the haul for what's left, and let a crew carry the heavy end.

Keep this list somewhere you can check things off. Crossing a line out is a small win on a hard day, and you'll want the wins.

## When there's a lot of accumulation

If the house has years of things stacked in it, understand this first: the person is not the problem. Accumulation is usually grief, or anxiety, or a life that got hard somewhere and never got easier. Shame doesn't clear a house. It just closes a door.

If the person is still living, go at their pace. Not yours. Never force a decision, and never throw something out behind their back. That's a trust you don't get back.

Aim for small wins. One corner. One closet. One clear path down a hallway. Let them see the floor again, and the next decision comes easier. Some families work through a [hoarding situation](/services/hoarder-cleanouts) over months. That's not failure. That's how it goes.

## When to call for help

Call when the pile is bigger than a car trunk. When there are stairs. When the appliances or the piano have to go. When your back has opinions. Or when you don't want to spend Saturday at the transfer station.

Also call when you're stuck. Sometimes a family stalls because nobody wants to be the one who threw out Dad's workbench. An outside crew makes that easier to live with.
